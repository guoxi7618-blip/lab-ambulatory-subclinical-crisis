[0] Laboratory Stress Reactivity and Ambulatory Monitoring in Subclinical Psychological Crisis Among University Students: A Preregistered Two-Semester Validation Study
[1] Abstract
[2] University mental health screening relies on self-report questionnaires that are vulnerable to concealment and provide static snapshots. In a preregistered two-semester study, we examined whether laboratory and ambulatory psychophysiology can complement self-report in identifying counselor-judged subclinical psychological crisis in university students. Participants completed a laboratory session with EEG, electrocardiography and electrodermal recording during rest and a campus-adapted stress task, followed by 30 days of wrist-worn monitoring and ecological momentary assessment (EMA). In the first semester (102 subclinical, 101 control), students with subclinical crisis showed lower resting vagally mediated heart rate variability, blunted heart rate reactivity, smaller vagal withdrawal and a flatter aperiodic EEG exponent; in the second semester (95 subclinical, 103 control), only the smaller vagal withdrawal replicated. Laboratory heart rate reactivity predicted daily-life responses to campus stressors (b = 0.16, p < 0.001), but anchoring ambulatory data to the laboratory baseline added little to identification (ΔAUC = 0.017, 95% CI [−0.012, 0.045]). A multiscale fusion model developed in the first semester and evaluated once in the second reached AUC = 0.73, comparable to the SDS/SAS (0.74); a simple model of physiological and wearable features performed similarly (0.74), and combining questionnaires with physiological and ecological information raised AUC to 0.79. Specificity was 0.51, and the expected positive predictive value at 18% prevalence was 0.26. Accuracy was lower during examination weeks. Ambulatory physiology and EMA can complement, but not replace, self-report in campus mental health services; a laboratory session is better justified for standardized marker assessment than for calibrating ambulatory data.

[5] 1 | Introduction
[6] University students carry a high burden of mental health problems. In the WHO World Mental Health International College Student project, 31% of first-year students screened positive for at least one common mental disorder in the previous 12 months (Auerbach et al. 2018), and risk profiles measured at university entry predict later onset of major depression (Ebert et al. 2019). Many universities, including most institutions in China, therefore run routine psychological screening. These programs rely almost entirely on self-report questionnaires, which have two weaknesses. Self-report is subjective: it is vulnerable to concealment and social desirability, particularly when students fear consequences for their academic record, and to recall and mood-congruent biases (Walsh et al. 2024). It is also static: questionnaires administered once or twice a year lag behind the fluctuations in affect and stress that follow the academic calendar (Myin-Germeys et al. 2018; Vidal Bustamante et al. 2022). Both weaknesses matter most for students whose difficulties have not yet reached a diagnostic threshold.
[7] 1.1 | Subclinical Psychological Crisis
[8] Subclinical states are common, impairing and prognostically important. Subthreshold depression affects roughly 11% of adults and is associated with impaired functioning, increased mortality and an elevated risk of future depressive episodes (Cuijpers et al. 2013; Furukawa et al. 2025), and psychological interventions reduce the incidence of major depression if those who would benefit are identified in time (Buntrock et al. 2024). Clinical staging models place such states at an early stage of a transdiagnostic continuum (McGorry et al. 2018). Consistent with dimensional models of psychopathology (Kotov et al. 2017), we use the term subclinical psychological crisis for clinically meaningful distress or crisis signals—such as acute stress reactions, functional impairment or passive suicidal ideation—that do not meet diagnostic criteria for a mental disorder. The term combines a severity criterion with a risk criterion that is relevant to campus mental health services. Because suicidal ideation can fluctuate within hours (Kleiman et al. 2017) and traditional risk factors predict suicidal outcomes only marginally better than chance (Franklin et al. 2017), measures that are repeated and less dependent on disclosure may complement infrequent self-report.
[9] 1.2 | Psychophysiological Markers of Stress Regulation
[10] Psychophysiological measures index stress-regulatory systems that are altered across the depression–anxiety spectrum and are less directly accessible to deliberate response distortion. The neurovisceral integration model links vagally mediated heart rate variability (vmHRV) to prefrontal regulation of emotion and stress (Thayer and Lane 2000), and resting vmHRV is reduced in major depression, most clearly for the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD; Hedges’ g = −0.46; Koch et al. 2019). Depression is also associated with atypical cardiac reactivity to, and recovery from, acute stress (Hamilton and Alloy 2016; Schiweck et al. 2019), and blunted stress reactivity has been linked to depression and motivational dysregulation (Phillips et al. 2013). Electrodermal activity (EDA) indexes sympathetic activation; both heightened electrodermal responses in young people who self-harm (Wetherall et al. 2025) and blunted physiological reactivity in people with higher depression and anxiety scores (Smets et al. 2018) have been reported. In resting EEG, frontal alpha asymmetry has not held up as a depression marker (van der Vinne et al. 2017), whereas the aperiodic (1/f) component of the power spectrum, which can be separated from oscillatory power (Donoghue et al. 2020), is altered in major depressive disorder (Woronko et al. 2025).
[11] These markers have two limitations for screening. Their effects are small and nonspecific: an umbrella review rated the credibility of HRV alterations in major depressive and generalized anxiety disorders below the “suggestive” level (Wang et al. 2025). And small effects estimated in samples of modest size are prone to inflation and replication failure (Marek et al. 2022), so markers identified in one sample need to be tested in another. Whether markers established for diagnosed disorders extend to subclinical states, and whether they replicate across independent samples, is largely unknown.
[12] 1.3 | From the Laboratory to Daily Life
[13] Standardized stress tasks yield precise and comparable reactivity measures, but whether laboratory responses generalize to daily life remains uncertain (Kidd et al. 2014; Zanstra and Johnston 2011). Ambulatory recordings capture daily life but lack standardization, and between-person differences in physiological levels and responses are large. In 1002 adults monitored for 5 days, group-level models detected daily-life stress with an F1 score of only 0.43, largely for this reason (Smets et al. 2018). In first-year students monitored during a high-stakes examination week, average physiological arousal unexpectedly decreased relative to a control week, a decrease mediated by lower positive affect, and individualized models outperformed group models (Tutunji et al. 2023). Daily-life heart rate (HR) and HRV also vary with posture and metabolic demand, which must be separated from psychologically meaningful changes (Schwerdtfeger and Rominger 2021).
[14] Ambulatory signals therefore appear to need a person-specific reference frame. A standardized laboratory session is one candidate: a person’s laboratory baseline and reactivity could anchor their ambulatory data. A person’s own ambulatory data are another, in line with idiographic approaches that model each person’s longitudinal data (Balliu et al. 2024; Fisher et al. 2018). To our knowledge, no study has tested whether laboratory measurements improve the interpretation of ambulatory physiology for mental health assessment. The question has practical weight, because a laboratory session is the most costly component of any assessment that includes one.
[15] 1.4 | Identification in Campus Settings
[16] Wearable-based detection studies typically contrast healthy volunteers with diagnosed patients, leaving people with mild or subclinical symptoms underrepresented (Hehlmann et al. 2026). The field is characterized by small samples—a median of 60.5 participants in a recent scoping review (Shen et al. 2025)—short follow-up and inconsistent reporting (De Angel et al. 2022). Because performance estimated within a single sample is optimistic, prediction models need validation in data collected separately from those used for development (Collins et al. 2024; Riley et al. 2020). Models trained on small samples generalize poorly: on multi-year passive-sensing data from 497 university students, depression-detection algorithms performed weakly across cohorts and years (Xu et al. 2022), mirroring a broader concern about the illusory generalizability of clinical prediction models (Chekroud et al. 2024). Two further questions are rarely addressed: whether objective measures add information beyond the questionnaires that services already use, and whether performance holds across the predictable structure of campus life—course and entrance examinations, job hunting and interpersonal stress in shared dormitories—which recurs on an academic calendar and in which interpersonal rather than academic stress may be especially predictive of later symptoms (Vidal Bustamante et al. 2022).
[17] 1.5 | The Present Study
[18] We combined a laboratory session with 30 days of ambulatory monitoring in university students whose status was determined by licensed counselors. In the laboratory, participants completed resting recordings and a campus-adapted Montreal Imaging Stress Task (Dedovic et al. 2005) while 32-channel EEG, electrocardiography (ECG) and EDA were recorded. During the following 30 days, they wore a wrist-worn sensor and completed ecological momentary assessment (EMA) that annotated academic, career and interpersonal stressors. Each participant’s laboratory baseline and reactivity, together with concurrent laboratory recordings from the wrist-worn device, were used to anchor that participant’s ambulatory data, and both data streams were integrated in a multiscale attention-based fusion model. The Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS) served as conventional comparators. A model developed in one semester was frozen and evaluated once in a sample recruited in the following semester, which provides a temporal test of generalization across cohorts and academic calendars. The design allowed us to ask, in the order of the preregistration, whether laboratory markers distinguish students with subclinical crisis, whether laboratory reactivity carries over to daily life and whether the combined information identifies these students in a new sample. We tested four preregistered hypotheses:
[19] H1 (laboratory markers). Compared with controls, students with subclinical psychological crisis show lower resting vmHRV, blunted HR reactivity and slower HR recovery during the laboratory stress task, as well as group differences in electrodermal reactivity (tested two-sided) and in resting EEG spectral features (exploratory).
[20] H2 (laboratory-to-field correspondence). Individual differences in laboratory stress reactivity are positively associated with physiological responses to campus stressors in daily life.
[21] H3 (identification validity). The multimodal fusion model identifies subclinical psychological crisis more accurately than single-modality models and the SDS/SAS, with performance retained in the independent validation sample.
[22] H4 (ecological stability). Identification performance remains stable across campus stress scenarios and time periods.
[23] Because anchoring ambulatory data to the laboratory adds a laboratory session to every assessment, we also quantified its contribution by comparing models with and without laboratory calibration. Analyses added after preregistration, including a replication of the laboratory markers in the second sample, are reported separately.
[24] 2 | Method
[25] 2.1 | Ethics and Risk Management
[26] The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the host university and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent, were informed that participation was voluntary and independent of academic evaluation, awards and financial aid, and could withdraw at any time without losing compensation already earned (compensation in Supporting Information S11). The stress task included a mild deception (fictitious peer performance) that was disclosed in a debriefing immediately after the session. Data were stored under pseudonymous codes by an independent data manager and were not shared with the university administration (Supporting Information S11). A risk management protocol was triggered by endorsement of PHQ-9 item 9 at screening, a positive Columbia–Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) screen during the clinical evaluation (Posner et al. 2011) or endorsement of the passive suicidal ideation item in the evening diary. A counselor contacted the participant within 24 h, and participants judged to be at high risk were referred on the same day to the university counseling center or a psychiatric hospital.
[27] 2.2 | Design, Preregistration and Participants
[28] The study used a two-sample observational design (Figure 1). A core sample was recruited in the autumn semester of 2025 for marker analyses and model development, and an extension sample in the following spring semester for temporal validation, a form of external validation in TRIPOD+AI terminology (Collins et al. 2024). Both samples completed online screening, a counselor clinical evaluation, a laboratory session (Day 0) and 30 days of ambulatory monitoring (Days 1–30); the core sample also completed a laboratory retest and a brief counselor re-evaluation on Day 31. Hypotheses, the sampling plan, primary outcomes and the analysis plan were preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) before data collection. Deviations are listed in Table S1; the most consequential were that invitations to the clinical evaluation were not restricted to the preregistered screening ranges, that the hyperparameters of the fusion model were fixed before analysis instead of being tuned in nested cross-validation, and that several analyses were added after preregistration (Section 2.8). Reporting follows STARD 2015 (Bossuyt et al. 2015), TRIPOD+AI (Collins et al. 2024) and the guidelines of the Society for Psychophysiological Research (Boucsein et al. 2012; Keil et al. 2014; Quigley et al. 2024).
[29] Participants were undergraduate and postgraduate students at a comprehensive university in East China. Data were collected from October 24, 2025 to January 17, 2026 (core sample) and from February 22 to May 16, 2026 (extension sample). Counselors and academic advisors did not recruit participants directly. The online screening comprised the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9; Kroenke et al. 2001) and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scale (GAD-7; Spitzer et al. 2006), chosen to differ from the SDS/SAS comparators. Students scoring 5–14 on the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 (possible subclinical status) and students scoring ≤ 4 on both (possible controls) were invited to the clinical evaluation; students scoring ≥ 15 on the PHQ-9 or endorsing item 9 were contacted under the risk management protocol, and students who had not consented to further contact were not invited. Contrary to the preregistered plan, invitations were not restricted to these ranges: students scoring ≥ 15 on the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 were also invited, so that participants were drawn from three screening strata (both scores ≤ 4; highest score 5–14; either score ≥ 15; Table S1, Figure S1). Screening scores were used only to invite participants; group membership was determined by the clinical evaluation alone. Eligible students were enrolled until a quota of 115 per group and sample was reached.
[30] Participants were full-time students aged 18–25 years without a current or past mental disorder, recent psychiatric or psychological treatment, medication affecting autonomic or central nervous system function, cardiovascular, neurological or endocrine disease, head injury, pregnancy or skin conditions precluding EDA recording (full criteria in Supporting Information S11). Students who met criteria for a mental disorder at the clinical evaluation were referred for care and not enrolled.
[31] The planned analyzable sample was 100 per group and sample. For H3, a validation sample of 100 cases and 100 controls yields a 95% confidence interval (CI) half-width of about ±0.06 for an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.80 (Hanley and McNeil 1982) (cf. Flint et al. 2021; Riley et al. 2020; Varoquaux 2018). For H1, 100 participants per group provide a power of about 0.90 for the meta-analytic resting RMSSD effect in major depression (g = 0.46; Koch et al. 2019; two-sided α = 0.05). For H2, the core sample provides 0.80 power for a between-person correlation of r ≈ 0.20.
[32] 2.3 | Laboratory Session
[33] Sessions took place between 13:30 and 17:30 in a sound-attenuated, temperature-controlled laboratory after abstention from alcohol, vigorous exercise, caffeine, nicotine and food (Supporting Information S11). The session comprised sensor preparation, adaptation (5 min), a resting baseline (4 min eyes closed and 4 min eyes open, counterbalanced), a control task (3 min), the stress task (11 min), recovery (10 min, analyzed as R1 = 0–5 min and R2 = 5–10 min) and a post-task rest (Figure 2A).
[34] Stress was induced with a campus-adapted Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST; Dedovic et al. 2005), chosen over speech-based paradigms because keypress responses minimize muscle artifacts and avoid confounding respiratory sinus arrhythmia by speech (Quigley et al. 2024). Mental arithmetic under an adaptive time limit was combined with a performance bar showing accuracy below a fictitious average of students in the same major, standardized negative feedback after each of three 3-min blocks and a visible camera; the control task used matched problems without time limit, feedback or comparison (details in Supporting Information S11). Subjective stress (0–100), state anxiety (six-item STAI; Marteau and Bekker 1992) and affect (I-PANAS-SF; Thompson 2007) were rated after the baseline (T1), after the stress task (T2) and after recovery (T3).
[35] EEG was recorded from 32 semi-dry electrodes at 500 Hz with an open-hardware amplifier based on the ADS1299 analog front end (Frey 2016; Marini et al. 2019) and streamed via the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL); because online impedance measurement was not available, signal quality was evaluated with signal-based criteria and alpha reactivity, using thresholds established in a pilot study (n = 12; Supporting Information S4). ECG was recorded at 1000 Hz in a modified lead II configuration, and respiration rate was estimated from the ECG-derived respiration signal (Makowski et al. 2021). EDA was recorded exosomatically with a constant-voltage (0.5 V) system (BIOPAC MP160 with EDA100C) from the thenar and hypothenar eminences of the non-dominant hand at 250 Hz (Boucsein et al. 2012). Participants also wore the ambulatory wristband, so that laboratory-grade and wrist-worn recordings could be mapped onto each other (hardware and synchronization in Supporting Information S11).
[36] 2.4 | Ambulatory Monitoring
[37] From Day 1 to Day 30, participants wore an Empatica EmbracePlus wristband on the non-dominant wrist day and night, recording blood volume pulse (64 Hz), EDA (4 Hz), skin temperature (1 Hz) and acceleration (64 Hz). A valid day required ≥ 16 h of wear including the main sleep period, and participants with ≥ 20 valid days were analyzed. EMA was delivered with m-Path (Mestdagh et al. 2023) as four semi-random prompts per day within fixed windows between 09:00 and 21:00 and an evening diary at 21:30. Prompts assessed stress, affect, stressful events and their type, activity and location; the evening diary included one passive suicidal ideation item, a positive response to which triggered the risk protocol (items in Supporting Information S11). Participants who completed ≥ 50% of prompts were analyzed; prior meta-analyses suggested compliance of 70%–78% (Vachon et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2017). Start dates were staggered so that each monitoring window contained a major campus stress event, such as examinations or the recruitment season, between Days 10 and 25 (cf. Tutunji et al. 2023).
[38] 2.5 | Reference Standard and Questionnaires
[39] Subclinical status was determined by one of four licensed counselors with at least 3 years of university counseling experience, supervised by a psychiatrist, who had been calibrated on 10 practice cases (κ ≥ 0.70). The semi-structured interview (median 52 min), held 1–3 days before the laboratory session, covered symptoms, functional impairment, stressors and suicide risk (C-SSRS) and reviewed DSM-5 criteria. Counselors were blind to physiological data and SDS/SAS scores. Each evaluation yielded a categorical judgment (no notable psychological distress, subclinical psychological crisis or probable mental disorder), a severity rating from 0 to 10 and, for the subclinical group, the type(s) of crisis signal. A second counselor rated audio recordings of 30% of core-sample and 10% of extension-sample evaluations. Following STARD (Bossuyt et al. 2015), we call the evaluation the reference standard, because it reflects professional judgment in university services rather than a structured diagnostic interview. Every evaluated student received one of the three judgments, and the models produced continuous probabilities, so there were no indeterminate reference-standard or index-test results.
[40] The SDS (Zung 1965) and SAS (Zung 1971), the instruments most widely used in Chinese university screening, were administered on Day 0 as comparators; Chinese norms define standard scores ≥ 53 (SDS) and ≥ 50 (SAS) as positive (Wang et al. 1999). Covariates included body mass index (BMI), handedness (Oldfield 1971) and menstrual cycle phase (Simon et al. 2021).
[41] 2.6 | Signal Processing and Features
[42] Bad EEG channels were identified from signal-based criteria and interpolated, transient artifacts were corrected with artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR), and artifactual components were removed after independent component analysis (ICA). Sessions with more than 20% unusable channels were excluded from EEG analyses. Interbeat-interval series were inspected and corrected before HRV analysis. Ambulatory HRV was computed only in sleep and rest windows after screening for non-wear and movement.
[43] In the laboratory, HR, RMSSD and high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV; 0.15–0.40 Hz, natural-log transformed) were computed for each phase; the low-frequency/high-frequency ratio was not used (Quigley et al. 2024). Reactivity was defined as stress minus control-task values and recovery as (stress − R2)/(stress − baseline), set to missing for low responders (stress − baseline < 1 bpm; < 0.05 μS for EDA). Tonic skin conductance level (SCL), nonspecific skin conductance response (NS-SCR) frequency and SCR amplitude were computed for the same phases. Resting EEG features were band power, individual alpha peak frequency (IAF), the aperiodic exponent and offset (specparam, 2–40 Hz; Donoghue et al. 2020), stress-related theta change and, as a secondary index, frontal alpha asymmetry (van der Vinne et al. 2017).
[44] Ambulatory features comprised 5-min and day-level cardiac, electrodermal, temperature, activity and sleep measures and EMA summaries (Supporting Information S11). EMA reports and calendar events were aligned with the preceding 30 min of physiology to assign scenario labels (academic–examination, academic–routine, career or entrance examination, interpersonal, no stressor) and time labels in a three-level annotation procedure (Figure 2B; Supporting Information S11).
[45] 2.7 | Identification Models
[46] The multiscale attention-based fusion (MSAF) model (Figure S2) encoded laboratory EEG and autonomic features and ambulatory data at the 5-min window, day and 30-day scales. A laboratory calibration module linked the two streams: ambulatory physiology was centered on the laboratory resting baseline and scaled by laboratory reactivity, with a device mapping derived from the concurrent laboratory recordings (anchored normalization), and laboratory features modulated the ambulatory encoder through feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM; Perez et al. 2018). A Transformer fused the modalities. To limit overfitting, the model had 57,507 parameters, the ambulatory encoder was pretrained on unlabeled core-sample windows, and hyperparameters were fixed before analysis (Supporting Information S5).
[47] Development used participant-level 5-fold cross-validation in the core sample, with an inner split used only for early stopping. The decision threshold was fixed as the threshold that maximized specificity subject to sensitivity ≥ 0.80. The final model was trained on the full core sample, and its weights and scripts were time-stamped on OSF on February 26, 2026, before the extension-sample laboratory sessions began and before an independent data manager released the extension-sample labels. The frozen model was evaluated once.
[48] Comparators were single-modality models, the SDS, the SAS and their combination, and a preregistered elastic-net baseline on 12 psychophysiological features. Ablations removed the calibration module (and, separately, anchored normalization and FiLM), the multiscale structure, the EEG input and the EMA input; a wrist-plus-EMA configuration required no laboratory session.
[49] 2.8 | Statistical Analysis
[50] Analyses used R and Python. Tests were two-sided (α = 0.05) unless stated otherwise, with false discovery rate (FDR) control within families.
[51] H1: laboratory markers. Cardiac and electrodermal measures were analyzed with linear mixed-effects models (group × phase, adjusted for sex, age and BMI). Group differences in 14 derived markers were tested with independent-samples t tests, FDR-corrected as one family and reported as Cohen’s d; EEG differences were also tested with cluster-based permutation tests (5000 permutations). A preregistered confirmatory composite (the mean of standardized resting ln RMSSD and HR reactivity, both reversed) was compared between groups in the pooled samples, one-sided. Test–retest reliability between Day 0 and Day 31 was quantified with ICC(3,1).
[52] H2: laboratory-to-field correspondence. Multilevel models predicted event-related ambulatory HR responses (the 30 min before prompts reporting a stressor, relative to the participant’s no-stressor windows) from laboratory reactivity. Secondary analyses tested mediation of examination-week changes in arousal by positive affect (Tutunji et al. 2023) and compared a preregistered baseline-deviation index (awake resting HR above, and nocturnal ln RMSSD below, the laboratory values) between groups, one-sided.
[53] H3: identification validity. Performance was summarized by discrimination, classification metrics, predictive values at the estimated screening prevalence of 18% and calibration, with bootstrap 95% CIs (2000 resamples). AUCs were compared with DeLong’s test (DeLong et al. 1988), and ablations with participant-level bootstrap CIs of the AUC difference. Incremental validity was tested with a model that combined the SDS/SAS with all physiological and ecological blocks. Because the reference standard recorded passive suicidal ideation, the EMA and fusion models were refitted without the EMA passive-ideation item.
[54] H4: ecological stability. Because the MSAF model yields one prediction per participant, an elastic-net model trained on core-sample person-days predicted each extension-sample day from that day’s wristband and EMA summaries and the participant’s laboratory features; each day inherited the participant’s label. Effects of scenario and time on prediction correctness were tested with generalized estimating equations.
[55] Participants were excluded if laboratory data were missing, fewer than 20 valid days were available or compliance was below 50%; remaining missing feature values were imputed within training folds. Sensitivity analyses are described in Supporting Information S11.
[56] Added analyses. The following analyses were added after preregistration and are exploratory (Table S1): (a) replication of the core-sample markers in the extension sample, with adjustment of reactivity for the control-task value (law of initial values); (b) identification from physiological input alone, from prompt compliance alone and with person-level elastic-net models trained on the core sample (Table S9); (c) discrimination by sex, graduating year and crisis-signal type, and the correlation of predicted probabilities with counselor-rated severity; (d) within-person calibration, in which each day from Day 8 onward was classified as above or below the participant’s median EMA stress from awake resting HR and nocturnal ln RMSSD, raw or anchored to the laboratory baseline or to the first ambulatory week; and (e) the share of between-participant variance in day-level predictions.
[57] 3 | Results
[58] 3.1 | Participants, Data Quality and Manipulation Check
[59] Of 2330 students who completed screening, 1620 were invited, 1370 attended the clinical evaluation, 80 were referred for a probable mental disorder and 460 were enrolled (Figure S1). Of the enrolled participants, 2 withdrew before the laboratory session, 54 had fewer than 20 valid wear days and 13 completed fewer than 50% of prompts (categories overlap). The analyzable core sample comprised 203 participants (102 subclinical, 101 control) and the extension sample 198 (95 subclinical, 103 control); by screening stratum, 58 had both scores ≤ 4, 288 a highest score of 5–14 and 55 a score ≥ 15. The groups did not differ in age, sex or BMI (Table 1). Controls spanned a broad range of screening symptoms (core: PHQ-9 median 4, range 0–13), whereas the subclinical group had a median of 11 (range 2–24), so the comparison was not an extreme-group design. Among subclinical participants, the most frequent crisis signals were acute stress reactions (55.3%), functional impairment (50.3%), anhedonia or social withdrawal (40.1%), passive suicidal ideation (30.5%) and self-harm thoughts or behavior (14.7%); 52.3% presented with a single signal. Agreement on the reference standard was κ = 0.83 and on the severity rating ICC(2,1) = 0.89. The risk protocol was triggered 519 times (288 at screening, 81 during the evaluation and 150 by the diary item), with a median time to contact of 10.3 h and 45 same-day referrals.
[60] Data quality is summarized in Table 2. Fifteen of 458 Day 0 EEG sessions (3.3%) were excluded, alpha reactivity exceeded 1 in 98.7% of sessions, and 12.0% of participants were electrodermal nonresponders. Participants had a median of 25 valid wear days and completed 76.6% of prompts; compliance was lower in the subclinical group (74.4% vs. 78.9%, p < 0.001; analyzable participants, 74.7% vs. 79.5%), so missingness itself could carry information. The median LSL clock offset was 4.2 ms. The MIST increased subjective stress from 27.6 at T1 to 59.0 at T2 (p < 0.001), with a return toward baseline at T3 (33.7), and increased HR by 8.7 bpm relative to the control task.
[61] 3.2 | Laboratory Markers (H1)
[62] On the preregistered autonomic composite, the subclinical group scored higher than controls (d = 0.36, 95% CI [0.17, 0.56], one-sided p < 0.001; pooled samples). In the core sample (Table 3; Figure 3), the subclinical group showed lower resting ln RMSSD (d = −0.35, 95% CI [−0.63, −0.07]) and ln HF-HRV (d = −0.34, 95% CI [−0.62, −0.06]), blunted HR reactivity (d = −0.34, 95% CI [−0.61, −0.06]) and smaller vagal withdrawal during stress (ln RMSSD reactivity, d = 0.59, 95% CI [0.31, 0.87]); the group × phase interactions were significant for HR (χ2(6) = 42.27, p < 0.001) and ln RMSSD (χ2(6) = 82.06, p < 0.001). All four differences survived FDR correction (pFDR ≤ 0.048). The HR recovery index did not differ (d = −0.19, pFDR = 0.282), so the hypothesized slower recovery was not supported. For SCL, the group × phase interaction was significant (χ2(6) = 32.71, p < 0.001); SCL reactivity and NS-SCR frequency during stress were lower in the subclinical group at the uncorrected level (d = −0.30 and −0.29) but not after correction (both pFDR = 0.073).
[63] For EEG (97 control and 100 subclinical participants), a cluster-based permutation test identified a negative cluster for the aperiodic exponent spanning all 32 channels (cluster p = 0.003), indicating a flatter spectrum in the subclinical group; the channel-averaged exponent differed after FDR correction (d = −0.42, 95% CI [−0.70, −0.14], pFDR = 0.025). No cluster emerged for the oscillatory bands, and posterior alpha power (d = 0.10), IAF (d = −0.12), frontal alpha asymmetry (d = −0.24, pFDR = 0.152) and frontal theta change (d = 0.17) did not differ. Between Day 0 and Day 31, reliability was ICC(3,1) = 0.66 for resting ln RMSSD, 0.75 for resting HR, 0.46 for HR reactivity, 0.61 for tonic SCL, 0.60 for posterior alpha power and 0.72 for the aperiodic exponent, and change in counselor-rated severity was not associated with change in resting ln RMSSD (r = 0.00). H1 was partially supported.
[64] 3.3 | Laboratory-to-Field Correspondence and Calibration (H2)
[65] Across 32,891 prompts from 401 participants, HR in the 30 min before prompts that reported a stressor was 1.48 bpm higher than before no-stressor prompts (95% CI [1.39, 1.57]). Participants with greater laboratory HR reactivity showed larger ambulatory responses (cross-level b = 0.16 bpm per SD, 95% CI [0.07, 0.25], p < 0.001; person-level r = 0.16; Figure 4A), and laboratory electrodermal reactivity was associated with ambulatory electrodermal responses (r = 0.22, p < 0.001). H2 was supported, although the associations were small.
[66] During examination weeks, awake resting HR was 0.84 bpm lower than in other weeks (p < 0.001); positive affect also decreased (a = −0.53), and the indirect effect through positive affect was −0.47 bpm (95% CI [−0.51, −0.43]), with a remaining direct effect of −0.37 bpm. On the preregistered baseline-deviation index, the subclinical group showed only marginally greater daily-life activation (d = 0.17, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.37]; 90% CI [0.01, 0.34]; one-sided p = 0.047), and the deviation increased only weakly with counselor-rated severity (r = 0.12). In both groups and every campus scenario, pre-prompt HR in daily life lay 4–6 bpm below the laboratory resting value (Figure 4B).
[67] Laboratory calibration contributed little to identification. Removing the calibration module reduced AUC by 0.017 (95% CI [−0.012, 0.045]; DeLong p = 0.273), removing anchored normalization or FiLM alone changed AUC by 0.009 and −0.004, and the laboratory-free wrist-plus-EMA configuration reached AUC = 0.70, 95% CI [0.62, 0.77] (Table 4).
[68] 3.4 | Identification Validity (H3)
[69] In 5-fold cross-validation in the core sample, the MSAF model reached AUC = 0.70, 95% CI [0.62, 0.77]. In the extension sample, evaluated once, it reached AUC = 0.73, 95% CI [0.66, 0.80], with accuracy 0.64, recall 0.77, specificity 0.51 and F1 score 0.67 at the preregistered threshold (Table 4; Figure 5A); it classified 73 of 95 subclinical participants and 53 of 103 controls correctly. The Brier score was 0.212, the calibration intercept 0.08 and the slope 0.82 (Figure 5B). At an estimated screening prevalence of 18%, the positive and negative predictive values were 0.26 and 0.91, so about three of four flagged students would not meet the reference standard.
[70] The MSAF model did not outperform the SDS/SAS (AUC = 0.74, 95% CI [0.67, 0.81]; DeLong p = 0.720). Combining the SDS/SAS with all physiological and ecological blocks raised AUC to 0.79 (DeLong z = 2.16, p = 0.031), with accuracy, recall and specificity of 0.72. Among single modalities, the wrist model reached AUC = 0.72, EMA 0.68, the laboratory autonomic model 0.65 and EEG 0.62 (Figure 5C); the MSAF model outperformed only the EEG model (p = 0.033). The wrist model was unstable across samples (development AUC = 0.55), and its threshold transferred poorly (recall = 0.23, specificity = 0.92). The preregistered elastic-net baseline reached AUC = 0.69. Removing EMA reduced AUC by 0.067 (95% CI [−0.002, 0.136]), the largest loss among the ablations, whereas removing EEG (0.011) or the multiscale structure (0.018) changed little. Integrated-gradients attributions were distributed across EMA (36.3%), laboratory EEG (31.7%), wristband (17.7%) and laboratory autonomic features (14.3%), but EEG attributions were largest at frontopolar sites, where residual ocular activity is most likely (Table S6b). Without the EMA passive-ideation item, the fusion model reached AUC = 0.73, 95% CI [0.66, 0.80]. In sensitivity analyses, AUC was 0.73 after excluding participants with < 70% compliance, 0.73 after temperature adjustment, 0.71 after within-sample standardization for season and 0.70 in pooled-sample cross-validation (Supporting Information S11). H3 was partially supported: performance was retained in the validation sample, but the fusion model was not more accurate than the questionnaires or the best single modalities.
[71] 3.5 | Ecological Stability (H4)
[72] Day-level balanced accuracy was 0.62 overall and ranged from 0.56 to 0.69 across scenario × time strata (Table 5; Figure 6A). Accuracy did not differ between weekdays and weekends (p = 0.436), across study weeks (OR = 1.01, p = 0.574) or by time of day (balanced accuracy 0.55–0.56; p = 0.131), but it differed by campus scenario (χ2(4) = 10.93, p = 0.027) and was lower during examination weeks (OR = 0.80, 95% CI [0.65, 0.97], p = 0.027). Within individual scenarios, however, examination weeks were not uniformly lower (Table 5). H4 was partially supported.
[73] 3.6 | Added Analyses
[74] Replication of laboratory markers. All five markers that survived correction in the core sample differed in the same direction in the extension sample, but only the smaller vagal withdrawal remained significant (ln RMSSD reactivity, d = 0.47, 95% CI [0.19, 0.76], pFDR = 0.005); effects were smaller for resting ln RMSSD (d = −0.17, 95% CI [−0.45, 0.11]), resting ln HF-HRV (d = −0.22, 95% CI [−0.50, 0.06]), HR reactivity (d = −0.19, 95% CI [−0.47, 0.09]) and the aperiodic exponent (d = −0.26, 95% CI [−0.55, 0.03]; all pFDR ≥ 0.185; Table S8). Adjusting reactivity for the control-task value changed the results little (core: HR reactivity −0.33, ln RMSSD reactivity 0.51; extension: ln RMSSD reactivity 0.44, 95% CI [0.16, 0.71]).
[75] Physiological information and compliance. Within the fusion model, the configuration without EMA reached AUC = 0.66, 95% CI [0.58, 0.74], below the SDS/SAS (DeLong z = −1.66, p = 0.098). Person-level elastic-net models used the same information better: physiological and wearable features alone reached AUC = 0.74, 95% CI [0.67, 0.81], comparable to the SDS/SAS (p = 0.96), and adding them to the SDS/SAS raised AUC to 0.78, 95% CI [0.72, 0.84] (ΔAUC = 0.042, p = 0.099; Table S9). Prompt compliance alone reached AUC = 0.66, 95% CI [0.59, 0.74], but added only 0.011 to a model that contained all other blocks (p = 0.17).
[76] Subgroups and heterogeneity. Discrimination was similar in women (AUC = 0.72, 95% CI [0.64, 0.81]) and men (0.72, 95% CI [0.60, 0.83]) and in graduating and non-graduating students (both 0.73). Against all controls, AUC was 0.86, 95% CI [0.78, 0.93], for subclinical participants with passive suicidal ideation (0.82 with the model refitted without the EMA passive-ideation item) and 0.69 for those without; it was 0.67, 95% CI [0.58, 0.75], for participants with a single crisis signal and 0.79, 95% CI [0.72, 0.86], for those with several. The SDS/SAS showed the same gradient. Predicted probabilities correlated with counselor-rated severity (Spearman ρ = 0.48, p < 0.001; within the subclinical group, ρ = 0.23, p = 0.027).
[77] Within-person calibration and day-level structure. In distinguishing each participant’s higher- from lower-stress days (3787 core and 3691 extension person-days), raw awake resting HR and nocturnal ln RMSSD reached a pooled AUC of 0.52; anchoring to the laboratory baseline did not change this (Δ = 0.002, 95% CI [−0.011, 0.012]), whereas anchoring to the first ambulatory week raised it to 0.54 (Δ = 0.026, 95% CI [0.010, 0.039]; Figure 6B). The mean within-person AUC was 0.55 for all three approaches. In the day-level model, 92% of the variance in predicted log-odds lay between participants, and 60% of participants received the same predicted class on every day.
[78] 4 | Discussion
[79] 4.1 | Principal Findings
[80] In a preregistered two-semester study, we examined laboratory markers of subclinical psychological crisis in university students, their correspondence with daily-life physiology and the identification of students with subclinical crisis. The study thus tested both a substantive question—which laboratory markers characterize subclinical crisis—and a methodological one—whether a laboratory session improves ambulatory assessment. Three findings stand out. First, students with subclinical crisis showed several laboratory markers of altered stress regulation, but only one—smaller vagal withdrawal during stress—replicated in an independent sample. Second, laboratory HR reactivity predicted HR responses to campus stressors in daily life, yet using the laboratory session to calibrate ambulatory data added little: a configuration without any laboratory input performed comparably, and a reference built from the first ambulatory week served each person better than the laboratory baseline. Third, multimodal information identified subclinical crisis about as well as the questionnaires that campus services already use and added to them when combined; a simple model of the physiological and wearable features did as well as the fusion model, and accuracy was lower during examination weeks, when monitoring may matter most.
[81] 4.2 | Laboratory Markers: What Replicated
[82] Lower resting vmHRV in the core sample is consistent with the neurovisceral integration model (Thayer and Lane 2000), and its size (d = −0.35) was numerically smaller than the meta-analytic effect in major depression (g = −0.46; Koch et al. 2019), compatible with a severity gradient. In the extension sample, however, the effect shrank to d = −0.17, as did the effects for HR reactivity and the aperiodic exponent. Shrinkage of this kind is expected when effects selected for significance in one sample of modest size are re-estimated in another (Marek et al. 2022), and it fits the conclusion of an umbrella review that HRV alterations in depression and anxiety are small and of limited credibility (Wang et al. 2025). The core-sample estimates should therefore be read as upper bounds.
[83] Smaller vagal withdrawal during stress replicated in both samples, was not explained by prestress levels and was the largest marker effect. Together with blunted HR reactivity in the core sample, it points to reduced autonomic responsiveness to a social-evaluative challenge rather than to heightened arousal, consistent with evidence that blunted stress reactivity accompanies depression and motivational dysregulation (Phillips et al. 2013; Smets et al. 2018) and with atypical vagal reactivity in depression (Hamilton and Alloy 2016). Why only vagal withdrawal replicated is uncertain. It was the largest marker effect in the core sample and therefore the least vulnerable to shrinkage, and, as a within-session change score, it is less sensitive than resting levels to between-person differences in baseline and to the conditions of a particular session; HR reactivity, also a change score, was a smaller effect and did not replicate. Because reactivity indices were only moderately reliable over 1 month (ICC = 0.46 for HR reactivity), however, a single session estimates them with error, which may have attenuated them in both samples; multiple sessions or reactivity aggregated across tasks would improve precision.
[84] The electrodermal pattern was weak; opposing directions reported for self-harm (Wetherall et al. 2025) and for withdrawn presentations (Smets et al. 2018) may cancel at the group level. The flatter aperiodic exponent in the core sample extends findings in major depressive disorder (Woronko et al. 2025) to subclinical states, but it did not replicate significantly and was recorded with an open-hardware amplifier, so it requires confirmation. Frontal alpha asymmetry was not a marker, consistent with van der Vinne et al. (2017).
[85] 4.3 | From the Laboratory to Daily Life
[86] Laboratory HR reactivity predicted HR responses to self-reported campus stressors, extending work on laboratory–field correspondence for cortisol and cardiovascular reactivity (Kidd et al. 2014; Zanstra and Johnston 2011) to a prompt-level design with self-reported campus stressors, but the association was small (r = 0.16), and the step that was meant to exploit it—anchoring ambulatory data to the laboratory—added little. Removing the calibration module changed AUC by 0.017, with an upper CI bound of 0.045, so small gains cannot be excluded; but a configuration without any laboratory input reached AUC = 0.70, and laboratory anchoring did not help to identify a person’s own higher-stress days.
[87] Two features of the data explain why. First, laboratory and daily-life conditions differ systematically: pre-prompt HR in daily life lay 4–6 bpm below the laboratory resting value in every scenario. The laboratory baseline was recorded shortly before a stress task in an unfamiliar setting and probably contained anticipatory arousal, whereas daily-life HR also varies with posture and metabolic demand (Schwerdtfeger and Rominger 2021). Second, a single laboratory session estimates a person’s baseline with error (ICC = 0.66 for resting ln RMSSD). A week of ambulatory data samples the conditions in which the target is measured and served as a better reference, a minimal form of the idiographic approach (Balliu et al. 2024; Fisher et al. 2018) that addresses the large between-person differences that have constrained ambulatory stress detection (Smets et al. 2018; Tutunji et al. 2023). Even so, all reference frames distinguished a person’s higher- from lower-stress days only marginally above chance (AUC = 0.52–0.55), so wrist-worn HR and HRV are not usable day-level stress indicators on their own.
[88] Daily-life arousal also did not map onto stress in a simple way. As in Tutunji et al. (2023), awake HR decreased during examination weeks, partly through lower positive affect, and the preregistered baseline-deviation index, motivated by the perseverative-cognition account (Brosschot et al. 2007; Ottaviani et al. 2016), separated the groups only marginally. Ambulatory physiology therefore needs psychological context, which EMA supplied: removing EMA was the largest loss among the ablations. The laboratory session remains valuable for what it does well—standardized, comparable measurement of reactivity markers—but our data do not support it as a calibration step for ambulatory monitoring. For ambulatory studies, a run-in week that establishes each person’s own baseline is a cheaper and, in our data, better-performing alternative.
[89] 4.4 | What Multimodal Assessment Adds to Self-Report
[90] The multimodal assessment matched but did not exceed the SDS/SAS, and combining it with them raised AUC from 0.74 to 0.79; it is therefore a complement to self-report rather than a replacement. This contribution needs three qualifications. First, it addresses the static nature of questionnaires more clearly than their subjectivity, because EMA is itself self-report and carried the largest share of performance. Second, the physiological information was present but not fully used by the fusion model: within the model, physiological input alone reached AUC = 0.66, whereas a person-level elastic-net model of the same features matched the questionnaires (AUC = 0.74). With 57,507 parameters trained on 203 participants, the fusion model offered no advantage over simpler models, in line with warnings about complex models in small clinical samples (Flint et al. 2021; Varoquaux 2018), and interpretable models may be preferable in practice. Third, compliance carried information on its own (AUC = 0.66) but little beyond the other blocks, so the models did not rely mainly on missing data. These results lie between earlier findings: wrist-worn EDA features distinguished university students with high and low perceived stress (Sano et al. 2018) and wearable data tracked depression severity across clinical and non-clinical samples (Hehlmann et al. 2026), whereas passive sensing added little to daily mood ratings in medical interns (Horwitz et al. 2023). In our data, physiological and wearable information was informative on its own and added to the questionnaires, but its added value was modest and did not reach significance in the person-level models.
[91] Performance also depended on the target condition. Discrimination was higher for students with passive suicidal ideation or several crisis signals and lower for the half with a single signal (AUC = 0.67), and predicted probabilities tracked counselor-rated severity. The categorical label therefore aggregates states that differ in how detectable they are, and model outputs may be more useful as graded risk estimates than as binary flags. The calibration slope of 0.82 indicates that the predicted probabilities were somewhat too extreme, so the model would need recalibration before its outputs are used as risk estimates. Performance did not decline across semesters (AUC = 0.70 in development and 0.73 in validation), but the CIs overlap widely, and a decline should be expected in other settings (Chekroud et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2022). For assessment research, the study offers a campus-adapted stress task, an annotation scheme that links physiological windows to campus scenarios and a validation design in which a model is frozen in one semester and evaluated once in the next.
[92] 4.5 | Implications for Campus Mental Health Services
[93] The results suggest a tiered use. Voluntary wrist-worn monitoring with brief EMA requires no laboratory session and could complement existing questionnaires, but its specificity at the preregistered threshold was only 0.41, so thresholds would need to be set for the intended use. A laboratory session may be reserved for standardized assessment of students with persistent risk signals. Three cautions apply. With a specificity of 0.51 and an expected positive predictive value of 0.26, most flagged students would not meet the reference standard, so flags should prompt a conversation, not a label. Accuracy was lower during examination weeks, when monitoring is arguably most needed. And voluntariness, separation of individual data from the university administration and a defined risk response are essential (Figueroa et al. 2025; Mohr et al. 2020; Walsh et al. 2024).
[94] 4.6 | Limitations
[95] Several limitations qualify these conclusions. First, the reference standard was a cross-sectional counselor evaluation rather than a structured interview; agreement was high (κ = 0.83), but the judgment was informed by students’ own reports and therefore shares method variance with the questionnaires, which favors them in head-to-head comparisons. Longitudinal outcomes that do not depend on self-report, such as help-seeking or functional decline, are needed. Second, the sample came from one university, invitations were not restricted to the preregistered screening ranges, probable disorders were excluded and both groups were recruited by quota, so the case mix and predictive values differ from those of a routine screening population. Third, season and sample were confounded, so temporal validation cannot separate effects of season, cohort and academic calendar; temperature adjustment and within-sample standardization changed AUC little (0.73 and 0.71). Fourth, the reference standard recorded passive suicidal ideation, which was also reported through EMA; the refit without this item gave the same AUC (0.73), but discrimination was highest in this subgroup. Fifth, EEG was recorded with an open-hardware amplifier without online impedance measurement, and wrist-worn signals have limited validity during movement (Menghini et al. 2019; van der Mee et al. 2021); removing EEG changed discrimination little, but the aperiodic findings require replication with research-grade amplifiers. Sixth, H4 was tested with a day-level model that included time-invariant laboratory features, so it mainly tested whether daily variation disrupted a stable person-level prediction. Finally, the replication of markers and several other analyses were added after the extension-sample labels had been released; they are exploratory and call for preregistered replication.
[96] Future work should test the replicated vagal-withdrawal marker and the ambulatory reference frame in preregistered, multi-site and multi-year samples; relate laboratory and ambulatory measures to outcomes that do not depend on self-report, such as help-seeking, academic functioning and later diagnosis; and compare person-level models with idiographic models that use each student’s own data as the reference (Fisher et al. 2018). Such studies should report graded risk estimates and their calibration in the target population rather than accuracy at a single threshold.
[97] 4.7 | Conclusion
[98] In university students, laboratory and ambulatory psychophysiology identified counselor-judged subclinical psychological crisis about as well as standard questionnaires in an independent semester and added to them when combined, and a simple model of the physiological and wearable features performed as well as a complex fusion model. Of several laboratory markers, only smaller vagal withdrawal during stress replicated. Laboratory reactivity predicted responses to campus stressors in daily life, but calibrating ambulatory data against the laboratory session added little, and a week of ambulatory data provided a better person-specific reference. For campus services, ambulatory monitoring with EMA can complement self-report and professional judgment without a laboratory session; the laboratory session is better justified for standardized assessment of psychophysiological markers.
