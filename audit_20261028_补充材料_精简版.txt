[54] S11 | Supplementary Methods and Results
[55] This section contains the detailed methods and additional results that were summarized in the main text. References cited here are listed in the main text.
[56] S11.1 | Ethics, Data Protection and Compensation
[57] Data protection and de-identification. Identifiable information was stored separately from research data by an independent data manager, and all data were collected, processed and stored under pseudonymous codes. Location was recorded only as a self-reported category, and no GPS data were collected. Individual-level data were not shared with the university administration. The reference-standard labels of the extension sample were held by the data manager until the model had been frozen. Data handling complied with China’s Personal Information Protection Law regarding sensitive personal and biometric information.
[58] Compensation. Participants received up to CNY 650 (core sample) or CNY 550 (extension sample). Compensation was prorated to completed components (laboratory session, clinical evaluation, valid wear days and completed prompts) and included a weekly bonus for ≥ 80% prompt completion. Withdrawal never reduced compensation already earned, and no component was conditional on completing the whole study.
[59] S11.2 | Eligibility
[60] Inclusion criteria were (a) age 18–25 years, (b) full-time enrollment and (c) normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria were (a) a current or past diagnosis of a mental disorder, or psychiatric or psychological treatment within the previous 6 months; (b) use of psychotropic or other medication affecting autonomic or central nervous system function (e.g., antidepressants, anxiolytics, beta-blockers) within the previous 3 months; (c) cardiovascular, neurological (including epilepsy) or endocrine disease; (d) a history of head injury with loss of consciousness; (e) pregnancy; and (f) skin conditions on the hands or wrists that precluded EDA recording. Subclinical status required clinically meaningful distress and/or crisis signals without meeting criteria for any mental disorder; students who met diagnostic criteria for a mental disorder at the clinical evaluation were referred for care and were not enrolled.
[61] S11.3 | Laboratory Session
[62] Setting and preparation. Sessions took place in a sound-attenuated, temperature-controlled laboratory (22 ± 1°C, 40%–60% relative humidity) between 13:30 and 17:30 to minimize circadian variation. Participants were instructed to abstain from alcohol and vigorous exercise for 24 h, from caffeine and nicotine for 3 h, and from food for 1 h before the session; compliance was verified and recorded on arrival. Participants sat upright with their feet flat on the floor throughout the session. EDA electrodes were attached to the non-dominant hand, and participants responded with the dominant hand.
[63] Procedure. The session comprised the following phases (Figure 2A): sensor preparation (approximately 15 min); adaptation (5 min); a resting baseline with 4 min eyes closed and 4 min eyes open while fixating a cross (order counterbalanced across participants); a control task (3 min); the stress task (11 min); recovery (10 min, eyes open, fixating a cross; analyzed as R1 = 0–5 min and R2 = 5–10 min); a post-task resting recording (2 min eyes closed, 2 min eyes open); and debriefing. Total EEG wearing time was approximately 55 min.
[64] Standardized stress task. Stress was induced with a campus-adapted version of the Montreal Imaging Stress Task (MIST; Dedovic et al. 2005). The MIST was chosen over speech-based paradigms because responses are given by keypress, which minimizes speech-related muscle artifacts in the EEG and avoids confounding respiratory sinus arrhythmia by speech (Quigley et al. 2024). The task was introduced as a “Mathematical Reasoning Aptitude Test for University Students.” Participants solved mental arithmetic problems under an adaptive time limit set approximately 10% below their running mean response time, which produced a high failure rate. A performance bar showed their accuracy as consistently below a fictitious “average of students in your major,” and after each of three 3-min blocks the experimenter delivered standardized negative feedback (e.g., “Your performance is below that of most students; please try to be faster”). A visible video camera added social-evaluative threat. In the control task, participants solved problems of matched difficulty without a time limit, performance feedback or peer comparison, which provided a baseline matched for motor and cognitive demands. Subjective stress (0–100 visual analogue scale), state anxiety (six-item State–Trait Anxiety Inventory; Marteau and Bekker 1992) and affect (International Positive and Negative Affect Schedule Short Form; Thompson 2007) were assessed after the resting baseline (T1), after the stress task (T2) and at the end of recovery (T3; affect at T1 and T3 only). The task was presented with PsychoPy.
[65] EEG recording. EEG was recorded with an ExpandableEEG 32-channel system, an open-hardware amplifier based on the Texas Instruments ADS1299 analog front end (24-bit resolution; manufacturer-specified input noise ≤ 1 μVpp; common-mode rejection ratio ≥ 110 dB). The system consists of a main acquisition board stacked with three expansion boards and is compatible with the OpenBCI Cyton protocol. Data were recorded at 500 Hz with a programmable gain of 24, transmitted over a dedicated Wi-Fi access point and simultaneously written to the on-board SD card as a backup. The amplifier was battery powered, isolating it from mains power. Semi-dry electrodes moistened with saline were positioned at 32 sites of the extended 10–20 system (Fp1, Fp2, AF3, AF4, F7, F3, Fz, F4, F8, FC5, FC1, FC2, FC6, T7, C3, Cz, C4, T8, CP5, CP1, CP2, CP6, P7, P3, Pz, P4, P8, PO3, PO4, O1, Oz, O2). The common reference (SRB2) was placed on the left earlobe and the bias (driven-ground) electrode on the right earlobe. Data were acquired with BrainFlow and streamed via the Lab Streaming Layer (LSL). Because the 32-channel configuration of this system does not currently support online impedance measurement, signal quality was evaluated with signal-based criteria, together with the eyes-closed/eyes-open alpha reactivity as a participant-level validity check. Packet loss was monitored online and quantified offline for each segment. The validity of open-hardware and wireless dry-electrode EEG systems relative to research-grade amplifiers has been reported previously (Frey 2016; Marini et al. 2019). A pilot study (n = 12) established the procedures and quality thresholds before data collection (Section S4).
[66] ECG recording. ECG was recorded with a separate 8-channel ExpandableEEG board in bipolar input mode, using disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes in a modified lead II configuration (below the right clavicle and on the left lower rib cage, with the ground on the right lower rib cage), sampled at 1000 Hz. Respiration rate was estimated from the ECG-derived respiration signal (Makowski et al. 2021) to verify that respiratory frequencies fell within the high-frequency band used for HRV quantification (Quigley et al. 2024).
[67] EDA recording. EDA was recorded exosomatically with a constant-voltage (0.5 V) system (BIOPAC MP160 with an EDA100C skin conductance amplifier; BIOPAC Systems, Goleta, CA, USA) using two reusable Ag/AgCl electrodes (BIOPAC TSD203; 6-mm contact diameter) filled with isotonic electrode paste (BIOPAC GEL101). The electrodes were attached to the thenar and hypothenar eminences of the non-dominant hand, and the signal was sampled at 250 Hz (Boucsein et al. 2012).
[68] Concurrent wrist-worn recording and synchronization. During the laboratory session, participants also wore the wrist-worn device used for ecological tracking on the non-dominant wrist. Simultaneous recordings from the laboratory-grade and wrist-worn sensors allowed a participant-specific mapping between the two device types, which formed part of the calibration procedure. Task events were sent from PsychoPy as LSL markers, and the EEG, ECG, EDA and marker streams were recorded with LabRecorder into a single XDF file. The wrist-worn device, which does not stream over LSL, was aligned offline using three brisk hand claps performed at the beginning and end of each session, which were identified in its accelerometer signal and in the marker stream. Because analyses were conducted on phase-level segments (≥ 1 min), a synchronization tolerance of < 50 ms was deemed sufficient; observed LSL clock offsets are reported.
[69] S11.4 | Ambulatory Monitoring
[70] Wearable monitoring. From Day 1 to Day 30, participants wore an Empatica EmbracePlus wristband on the non-dominant wrist 24 h per day, including during sleep, removing it only for bathing and charging. The device recorded blood volume pulse (64 Hz), EDA (4 Hz), skin temperature (1 Hz) and triaxial acceleration (64 Hz). Participants synchronized the device every 3 days; research assistants checked data completeness on the same day and contacted participants within 24 h when data were missing (cf. Sano et al. 2018). A valid day was defined as ≥ 16 h of wear time including the main sleep period. Participants with ≥ 20 valid days were included in ecological analyses.
[71] Ecological momentary assessment. EMA was delivered through the m-Path platform (Mestdagh et al. 2023). Four semi-random prompts per day were scheduled within fixed windows (09:00–11:00, 12:00–14:00, 15:00–17:00 and 19:00–21:00), and an evening diary was delivered at 21:30. Each prompt could be answered within 30 min, with a reminder after 15 min; participants could postpone a prompt during class, and postponement was recorded. Each prompt took 1–2 min and assessed current stress (0–100 slider); positive affect (happy, relaxed, energetic) and negative affect (down, tense/anxious, irritated), each on 7-point scales; whether a stressful event had occurred since the previous prompt, its type (examination; coursework; graduate-entrance or civil-service examination preparation; job hunting or internship; interpersonal [classmates, roommates, partner]; family; financial; health; other) and its severity (1–7); current activity; and location category. The evening diary assessed the most stressful event of the day, its type, coping and rumination (two items), and included one passive suicidal ideation item (“Today, did you have thoughts that life was not worth living or that you would be better off dead?”). A positive response automatically triggered the risk management protocol. Sleep quality for the previous night was rated at the first prompt of each day. On the basis of prior meta-analyses (Vachon et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2017), we expected compliance of 70%–78%. Participants who completed ≥ 50% of prompts were included in analyses involving EMA data.
[72] Alignment with the academic calendar. Start dates were staggered so that each participant’s 30-day window contained at least one major campus stress event between Days 10 and 25. This provided a pre-event baseline of at least 9 days and a post-event recovery period, and thereby a within-person contrast between stress and control periods (cf. Tutunji et al. 2023). In the core sample (autumn semester 2025), the events were the final examination period, the National Postgraduate Entrance Examination and coursework deadlines. In the extension sample (spring semester 2026), they were midterm examinations, the spring recruitment season and postgraduate admission interviews.
[73] S11.5 | Signal Processing, Annotation and Features
[74] Signals were processed with established software following the reporting guidelines of the Society for Psychophysiological Research for EEG (Keil et al. 2014), heart rate variability (Quigley et al. 2024) and electrodermal activity (Boucsein et al. 2012). EEG channel quality was assessed from the proportion of saturated (“railed”) samples, line-noise ratio, channel-wise correlation and high-frequency noise. Bad channels were interpolated, transient high-amplitude artifacts were corrected with artifact subspace reconstruction (ASR), and artifactual components were removed after independent component analysis (ICA). Laboratory sessions in which more than 20% of EEG channels were unusable were excluded from EEG analyses, and resting conditions with less than 120 s of artifact-free EEG were treated as missing. Interbeat-interval series were inspected and corrected before HRV analysis. Wristband data were screened for the artifacts typical of campus routines: non-wear periods, movement while walking between classes or during physical-education sessions (identified from acceleration), and signal loss during prolonged sedentary study. HRV was computed only in sleep and rest windows, and each 5-min window was labeled usable, questionable or unusable. Recovery indices were set to missing for low responders (stress − baseline < 1 bpm for heart rate; < 0.05 μS for electrodermal recovery).
[75] Data were annotated at three levels (Figure 2B). Level 1 (signal quality) was automatic: each segment was labeled usable, questionable or unusable according to the criteria above. Level 2 (context) combined automatic pre-annotation with review by trained research assistants. EMA reports (stressor type, activity, location) and academic-calendar events were aligned with physiological windows to assign scenario labels (academic–examination, academic–routine, career or entrance examination, interpersonal, no stressor) and time labels (morning, afternoon, evening, night; weekday vs. weekend; examination vs. non-examination week). Research assistants also adjudicated Level 1 segments labeled questionable. The 30 min of physiological data preceding each prompt response were assigned to that prompt; when a prompt was missing, scenario labels were inferred from calendar events and flagged as inferred. Level 3 (state) comprised the participant-level reference standard and day-level annotation labels identifying high-stress days (daily mean stress > 1 SD above the participant’s mean), with borderline cases adjudicated by an expert. These day-level annotation labels were not used as the outcome of the within-person calibration test, which dichotomized days at each participant’s median stress to obtain balanced classes within every participant. Inter-annotator agreement was assessed on a double-annotated 10% of Level 2 windows and is reported as Cohen’s κ; Level 3 agreement is reported in Section 3.1 of the main text.
[76] Laboratory cardiac features. Heart rate (HR), RMSSD and high-frequency HRV (HF-HRV; 0.15–0.40 Hz, natural-log transformed) were computed for the resting baseline (eyes open), the control task, each stress block and each recovery segment (R1, R2). Following Quigley et al. (2024), the low-frequency/high-frequency ratio was not used as an index of sympathetic activity. Reactivity was defined as stress minus control-task values and recovery as (stress − R2)/(stress − baseline). Because this ratio is unstable when the stress response approaches zero, the recovery index was set to missing for low responders.
[77] Laboratory electrodermal features. Tonic skin conductance level (SCL), non-specific skin conductance response (NS-SCR) frequency and mean SCR amplitude were computed for the same phases, together with reactivity and recovery indices defined as above.
[78] Laboratory EEG features. For each channel and resting condition, absolute and relative power were computed for the delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) bands (Welch’s method, 2-s Hann windows, 50% overlap). We also computed individual alpha peak frequency (IAF), alpha reactivity (eyes-closed/eyes-open alpha power), and the aperiodic exponent and offset (specparam, 2–40 Hz, fixed aperiodic mode; Donoghue et al. 2020). Frontal and parietal theta and alpha power changes were computed for the stress task relative to the control task. Frontal alpha asymmetry (ln[F4] − ln[F3] alpha power) was computed as a secondary index, given its limited meta-analytic support (van der Vinne et al. 2017).
[79] Ecological features. Window-level features (5-min windows) were resting HR, blood-volume-pulse-derived RMSSD, SCR frequency, skin temperature and the Euclidean norm minus one (ENMO) of acceleration. Day-level features were nocturnal RMSSD, resting HR, step count, sleep duration, sleep midpoint and sleep regularity. Across the 30 days, interdaily stability and intradaily variability of the rest–activity rhythm were computed. EMA features were the daily mean, within-day variability and inertia (autocorrelation) of stress, positive affect and negative affect, and the frequency of each stressor type.
[80] The SDS (Zung 1965) and SAS (Zung 1971), the instruments most widely used in Chinese university screening, served as conventional comparators. Each contains 20 items rated on a 4-point scale; raw scores were converted to standard scores. Chinese norms define standard scores of ≥ 53 (SDS) and ≥ 50 (SAS) as positive (Wang et al. 1999). The SDS and SAS were administered on Day 0 (and on Day 31 in the core sample) by a research assistant other than the evaluating counselor and were not used for group assignment. Covariates assessed on Day 0 included handedness (Edinburgh Handedness Inventory; Oldfield 1971), body mass index (BMI), smoking status, habitual caffeine intake, weekly physical activity, year of study, academic discipline and date of last menstrual period (Simon et al. 2021).
[81] S11.6 | Multiscale Attention-Based Fusion Model
[82] Architecture. The multiscale attention-based fusion (MSAF) model comprised four components (Figure S2). (a) Modality encoders: laboratory EEG features were represented as one token per channel with a learned channel-position embedding and encoded by a two-layer Transformer, and laboratory autonomic features were encoded by a multilayer perceptron. (b) Multiscale temporal encoding: ecological data were encoded at three time scales. At the window scale, sequences of 5-min window features were processed by a one-dimensional convolutional network with attention pooling to yield within-day representations. At the day scale, these were combined with the same day’s EMA features to form day tokens. At the multi-day scale, the 30 day tokens, with calendar embeddings (examination week, weekday), were processed by a Transformer. (c) Laboratory calibration module: this module linked the two tracks. In anchored normalization, each participant’s ambulatory physiological features were centered on their laboratory resting baseline and scaled by their laboratory stress-reactivity range, and a participant-specific mapping derived from the concurrent laboratory-grade and wrist-worn recordings corrected device-related offsets. In conditional modulation, laboratory features were encoded into a participant calibration vector that modulated the ecological encoder through feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM; Perez et al. 2018). (d) Cross-modal fusion: representations from the four modalities, with learned modality embeddings, were fused by a Transformer encoder. The fused representation fed a primary classification head (subclinical psychological crisis vs. control) and an auxiliary regression head predicting the counselor severity rating; multitask training was used to stabilize learning in a modest sample. Missing modalities were handled with modality dropout during training and learned mask tokens at inference.
[83] To limit overfitting, the model was constrained to approximately 100,000 parameters (final model: 57,507 trainable parameters, model width 32). The ecological encoder was pretrained with self-supervised masked reconstruction on unlabeled core-sample windows, without access to labels or to the extension sample. Hyperparameters were fixed before analysis; this replaced the preregistered nested cross-validation with an inner hyperparameter search (Table S1). Implementation details (layer sizes, optimizer, pretraining and attribution settings) are given in Section S5.
[84] Training and validation. Model development used the core sample only, with 5-fold cross-validation stratified by group. All splits were made at the participant level, so that no participant contributed data to both training and test folds. Because hyperparameters were fixed before analysis (model width 32; AdamW with a learning rate of 10−3 and weight decay of 10−2; batch size 24; modality dropout 0.15; masked-reconstruction pretraining for 6 epochs with a 15% mask rate), no hyperparameter search was performed and the cross-validation was not nested. Within each training fold, a stratified 20% inner validation split was used only for early stopping (maximum 80 epochs, patience 12). Class-weighted loss was used, and predictions were averaged over five random seeds. The decision threshold was fixed during development as the threshold that maximized specificity subject to sensitivity ≥ 0.80. The final model was then trained on the full core sample, and its weights and analysis scripts were time-stamped on OSF on February 26, 2026, before the extension-sample laboratory sessions began and before the independent data manager released the reference-standard labels of the extension sample. The frozen model was evaluated once on the extension sample.
[85] Comparison models and ablations. The MSAF model was compared with (a) single-modality models (laboratory EEG, laboratory autonomic, wrist physiology and EMA features); (b) the SDS, the SAS and their combination in a logistic regression; and (c) an interpretable baseline consisting of an elastic-net logistic regression on 12 preregistered psychophysiological features. Ablations removed the calibration module (and, separately, anchored normalization and FiLM), the multiscale structure (replaced by 30-day means), the EEG input and the EMA input. To inform implementation, we also evaluated a wrist-worn-plus-EMA configuration that requires no laboratory session. Feature contributions were estimated in the extension sample from the attention of the fusion token to each modality and with integrated gradients (24 steps, baseline = core-sample mean), and were mapped back to specific physiological measures, EEG channels and EMA items.
[86] S11.7 | Missing Data and Sensitivity Analyses
[87] Missing data. Participants were excluded from the primary analyses if laboratory data were missing, fewer than 20 valid wear days were available or EMA compliance was below 50%. Linear mixed models were estimated by maximum likelihood using all available observations. In the identification models, remaining missing feature values were imputed with the training-fold median, with missingness indicators added, within each cross-validation fold; in the MSAF model, missing modalities were handled with modality dropout during training and learned mask tokens at inference.
[88] Sensitivity analyses. Sensitivity analyses (a) excluded participants with < 70% EMA compliance, (b) adjusted for menstrual cycle phase, (c) varied the SDS/SAS cutoffs, (d) adjusted for season and daily ambient temperature, and (e) repeated the analyses in the core sample only and in the pooled samples.
[89] Excluding participants with less than 70% compliance yielded AUC = 0.73. Adjusting wrist features for ambient temperature yielded AUC = 0.73; because semester and sample coincided, season was also addressed by standardizing all features within sample, which yielded AUC = 0.71. The core sample alone (5-fold cross-validation) gave AUC = 0.70, and 5-fold cross-validation in the pooled samples (n = 401) gave AUC = 0.70. In women, the group difference in resting ln RMSSD after adjustment for menstrual cycle phase was b = −0.14 (p = 0.061, n = 123); in the pooled sample, it was b = −0.11 (p = 0.012). Varying the SDS cutoff from 50 to 56 traded sensitivity (0.56 to 0.42) against specificity (0.77 to 0.88).
[90] S11.8 | Additional Results
[91] Data quality indices are summarized in Table 2 of the main text. EEG packet loss had a median of 0.41% (range 0.02%–4.40%). A median of 1 channel (IQR 1–2) was interpolated and a median of 6 independent components were removed per session; 15 of 458 Day 0 sessions (3.3%) and 6 of 217 Day 31 sessions were excluded for having more than 20% bad channels, and ASR corrected a median of 3.0% of the data (IQR 1.8%–4.7%). Alpha reactivity exceeded 1 in 98.7% of sessions. In 99.9% of ECG analysis windows, fewer than 5% of beats required correction, and 12.0% of participants were electrodermal non-responders during the stress task. The median LSL clock offset was 4.2 ms (maximum 19.0 ms).
[92] Participants had a median of 25 valid wear days (IQR 23–27) and completed 76.6% of prompts on average across the 455 enrolled participants with ambulatory data (SD = 10.6). Across all enrolled participants, 40,053 of 52,260 scheduled semi-random prompts were completed (76.6%); among analyzable participants, 37,077 of 48,056 were completed (77.2%), and 32,891 completed prompts with valid physiology in the preceding 30 min entered the laboratory-to-field analysis. Compliance was lower in the subclinical group than in controls (74.4% vs. 78.9%; analyzable participants, 74.7% vs. 79.5%, p < 0.001). Level 2 context annotation reached κ = 0.73.
[93] Integrated-gradients attributions in the extension sample were distributed across modalities as follows: EMA 36.3%, laboratory EEG 31.7%, wristband 17.7% and laboratory autonomic 14.3%. The fusion token attended to the modalities in similar proportions (EMA 0.29, wristband 0.26, laboratory autonomic 0.23, laboratory EEG 0.21). The largest single-feature attributions were for daily interpersonal stressors, daily negative affect, resting EEG alpha power, the daily number of completed prompts and stress-related theta change (Table S6a). The contribution of prompt completion indicates that compliance itself carried information about group status: in an added analysis, prompt compliance alone discriminated the groups with AUC = 0.66, 95% CI [0.59, 0.74], in the extension sample (0.64 in the core sample), comparable to the laboratory autonomic model; added to a person-level model that contained all other blocks, however, compliance changed AUC by only 0.011 (DeLong p = 0.17; Table S9). EEG attributions were largest at frontopolar sites (Fp1, Fp2; Table S6b), where residual ocular activity is most likely, and removing EEG changed AUC only slightly; the EEG attribution share should therefore not be read as evidence of a cortical contribution.
